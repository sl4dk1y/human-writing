# The catalogue

Structural habits first, because they survive model updates. Word lists live in the language packs; they rot within months.

Everything here assumes you already know what the text is for. A pattern is a defect when it replaces content, and an asset when the author chose it. The test is always the same: remove it and see whether the reader lost a fact.

## Staging instead of stating

### Not X but Y

"not just... but", "not only... but also", "it's not X, it's Y", "X rather than Y", and the version split across sentences: "This doesn't mean X. It means Y."

The negative half invents a position nobody holds, which makes the positive half sound bigger than it is. Keep a contrast when it corrects something the reader actually believes, or when both halves carry information.

> Before: It's not just a note-taking tool, it's a second brain.
> After: Notes taken in the tool are searchable from the phone.

> Before: This does not mean every choice is equal. It means no outside system confirms which choice is right.
> After: No outside system confirms which choice is right, although the choices still differ in consequence.

The reversed form is just as common: "less like a filing cabinet and more like a conversation". It reads as an aphorism and carries the same empty comparison.

### One-line closers

A one-sentence paragraph restating the paragraph above: "That's the real win." "That distinction matters." "Read that again." Or the same closer after every section. Or a sentence after an example that names what the example showed: "This shows the importance of preparation."

Cut it unless the short sentence carries a fact of its own. A row of fragments ("No aesthetics. No nostalgia. No plan.") gets merged into one sentence with a specific claim.

### Staged openers

"Let's dive in." "Here's what you need to know." "Let's break this down." "Honestly?" "The thing is." "Look."

The text announces the point instead of making it. A casual "honestly" inside a sentence is ordinary speech; the tell is the standalone opener before a routine claim.

> Before: Let's dive into how caching works in Next.js. Here's what you need to know.
> After: Next.js caches data at several layers: request memoization, the data cache, the router cache.

### Arguing with nobody

"This isn't about X." "I'm not saying Y." "To be clear." "Don't get me wrong." "A tempting approach would be..." "One might think... but."

Someone is defending against an objection that appears nowhere, usually a leftover from an earlier draft. If the defence holds a real claim, state the claim.

> Before: This isn't mainly about prompt length, and I'm not arguing that documentation doesn't matter. The issue is whether the agent can use the instruction when it acts.
> After: The issue is whether the agent can use the instruction when it acts.

### Aphorisms that sound deep

"The real question is..." "At its core..." "What really matters..." "X is the language of Y." "X becomes a trap." "The currency of trust." "X is not a tool but a mirror."

An ordinary point dressed as a hidden truth. The dressing adds no detail, and the detail is the only thing that would make it worth reading.

> Before: Symmetry is the language of trust. Efficiency becomes a trap when teams forget the human layer.
> After: Symmetric layouts usually feel more predictable. Teams do over-optimise workflows and do miss how people use them.

## Rhythm by rule

### Forced triads

Three items to sound complete: "innovation, inspiration, and industry insights". Three parallel examples. Three short facts followed by a lesson. Also the paragraph-scale version: three miniature stories that each end the same way.

Check whether the third item adds anything. Usually it does not, and two items or one developed example says more. Keep three when the meaning has three parts.

> Before: The event features keynote sessions, panel discussions, and networking opportunities. Attendees can expect innovation, inspiration, and industry insights.
> After: The event has talks and panels, plus time for informal networking between sessions.

### Repeated openings

Several sentences in a row starting with the same subject, because repetition was handled by rule instead of by ear. Merge them, change the subject, or start with the action. Deliberate anaphora ("She came. She saw.") is the exception, and it is rare.

### Dashes as the universal connector

A dash lets the writer skip deciding how two clauses relate, so a model reaches for it everywhere. Replace it with a period, comma, colon or brackets, or rewrite the sentence.

Two notes. Editors use dashes too, so a single one is a weak signal, and in 2026 the models have moved below the human rate anyway (see `evidence.md`). Judge density and function: the tell is a dash doing the job of a comma in every paragraph. And in some languages the dash is required grammar, so read the language pack before you touch it.

### Stacked qualifiers

"to be fair", "it's also possible", "could potentially", "might arguably", "in some cases it may". Usually the residue of editing an overstatement down, one qualifier at a time. Keep one if the source supports real doubt. Ordinary hedges such as "perhaps" or "tends to" are human habits and not tells.

### Hyphenated pairs everywhere

Compound modifiers keep their hyphen in every position. Keep it before a noun ("a high-quality report") and drop it after ("the report is high quality"), except where the dictionary always hyphenates. Weak alone.

### Passive voice and missing subjects

He hides who acts: "No configuration file needed. The results are preserved automatically." Prefer the actor when the actor matters. Weak alone, and perfectly fine when the actor genuinely does not matter.

## Inflation and borrowed authority

### Inflated significance

"stands as a testament", "a pivotal moment", "plays a key role", "underscores its importance", "reflects a broader movement", "leaving a lasting legacy", "setting the stage for", "the future looks bright".

It appears at three scales: a phrase, a stock "Challenges and Legacy" section, and a send-off paragraph. Keep the fact, drop the significance, end on the last concrete thing. If the source states real plans, use those.

> Before: Despite industrial growth, Korattur faces the challenges typical of urban areas. Despite these challenges, with its strategic location and ongoing initiatives, Korattur continues to thrive as an integral part of Chennai's growth.
> After: Korattur has recurring traffic congestion and water shortages.

### Vague association

"associated with", "connected to", "linked to", "in connection with" without saying how. Was he the chief executive, a board member or a caterer? Name the relationship the source gives. If the source does not say, keep the vague wording rather than inventing a role.

### Shallow -ing riders

", highlighting...", ", underscoring...", ", emphasising...", ", ensuring...", ", reflecting...", ", fostering...", ", showcasing...". A participle bolted onto a plain fact to make it feel analysed. Attaching it to a named source ("Roger Ebert highlighted the lasting influence") does not make it true.

Russian does the same thing with деепричастные обороты; German with "wodurch ... wird"; Spanish with the gerundio; French with "permettant de". Same habit, different grammar.

> Before: The temple's palette of blue, green and gold resonates with the region, symbolising Texas bluebonnets and the Gulf of Mexico, reflecting the community's deep connection to the land.
> After: The temple is painted blue, green and gold, colours meant to evoke Texas bluebonnets and the Gulf of Mexico.

### Sales language

"rich", "profound", "nestled", "in the heart of", "groundbreaking", "renowned", "breathtaking", "must-visit", "vibrant", "stunning". State what the thing is.

> Before: Nestled in the breathtaking Gonder region of Ethiopia, Alamata Raya Kobo is a vibrant town with a rich cultural heritage.
> After: Alamata Raya Kobo is a town in the Gonder region of Ethiopia.

### Borrowed authority

"experts argue", "observers have cited", "industry reports", "some critics", plus the prestige list: "cited in The New York Times, the BBC, the Financial Times and The Hindu", and "over 500,000 followers".

An unnamed authority stands in for what was said. A list of outlets props up a person. Use the source, or cut the claim. A missing citation on its own is not a tell: most writing is unsourced.

### Avoiding is, are, has

"serves as", "stands as", "functions as", "operates as", "marks", "represents", "boasts", "features", "maintains" where "is" or "has" would do. Say the simple thing.

## Formatting by rule

### Bold as decoration

Words bolded without a reason, and vertical lists where every item gets a bold label and a colon. Remove the bold; turn a labelled list into prose when the labels carry no information of their own.

In measured corpora, bold appears about 43 times more often in machine text than in human text, headers 23 times, and any markdown at all 12 times. Those are the largest ratios found, which is why a document full of bold is a stronger signal than any single word in it.

> Before: It blends **OKRs**, **KPIs** and visual tools such as the **Business Model Canvas**.
> After: It blends OKRs, KPIs and visual tools like the Business Model Canvas.

### Decorative headings

Headings in Title Case, emoji or arrows as decoration, a horizontal rule between every section, and a document that opens with a heading repeating its own title. Also a heading written for effect ("The decision, on one screen") where the reader needs a label ("How the six options compare").

### Curly quotes in the wrong place

Typography from a word processor pasted into plain text: “curly” quotes where the file uses straight ones, non-breaking spaces, mixed dash types, "≈" and "→" in running prose. Weak alone, strong in company.

## Leftovers

### Chatbot residue

"I hope this helps!" "Great question!" "You're absolutely right." "Would you like me to..." "Let me know if you'd like more detail." A greeting, a compliment, an offer or a sign-off that belongs in a chat window and not in the document. Remove the wrapper, keep the content. This is the most certain tell on the list and the easiest to miss when it wraps real material.

### Knowledge-limit disclaimers

"As of my last training update", "based on the available information", "specific details are limited", "not widely documented", followed by a confident guess dressed as a hedge. State what the source does not show, or cut the sentence.

### Writing about the document

Describing how the text was made, what it replaced, how it was sourced, or what the reader can already see ("the table below compares"). Keep a source credit the reader can follow. Cut the account of the work. Mention a previous version only in a changelog or a migration guide.

### A heading repeated in the first sentence

"## Performance" then "Speed matters." then the content. Delete the middle line.

## Writing for the wrong reader

A model writes for a reader with no context, because that fits the widest range of cases. In a reply, the reader already knows the background.

The symptom: a short reply that restates the problem, walks through the diagnosis, lays out the evidence, and reaches the decision in the last line. Each sentence reads fine on its own, which is why sentence-level cleanup misses it.

Lead with the decision. Keep the reasoning that would change whether the reader agrees, usually one fact they lack plus anything they need to act.

> Before: Yeah, you're right, this works around the issue rather than fixing it. The real fix is in MergeService: when we move a child under a new parent, it should update pipeline_id along with parent_id. We can backfill from the audit log. I checked QA: 123 past merges, only 6 rows wrong now. Since MergeService is shared, I'd rather open a separate ticket than widen this PR.
> After: Agreed, this is a workaround. Fixing it properly means changing shared code, so it widens the ticket, plus a backfill for the rows already wrong. I'd rather keep this one account specific and open a separate ticket for MergeService.

## What human writing does

The same corpus work that identified the machine habits also found what humans do more often, and this half is more useful than the list of bans.

- Speaks in the first person and addresses the reader directly.
- Uses contractions, which models increasingly avoid.
- Drops casual qualifiers: "pretty good", "sort of".
- Tells small anecdotes and refers to personal experience.
- Uses exclamation marks more than a hundred times as often as current models.
- Interrupts itself with parenthetical asides far more often.
- Ends on a detail instead of a summary.
- Has uneven sentence lengths, and occasionally a sentence that runs on because the thought ran on.

One counterintuitive finding: fully generated text has higher lexical diversity and higher entropy than human writing. A rich vocabulary is not a human signature. A concrete detail is.
